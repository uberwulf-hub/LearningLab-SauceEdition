# Unit: u04 | 4. Threat actors, vectors and social engineering | Threats, vulnerabilities and mitigations

To defend well you need to know who might attack, why, and how they get in. This unit describes the kinds of **threat actors**, the attributes that set them apart and what drives them; the **threat vectors** and **attack surfaces** they use, from email to open ports to suppliers; and the **social engineering** tricks that target people rather than machines.

A **threat actor** is the person or group behind an attack. A **threat vector** (or attack vector) is the path they use to get in. The **attack surface** is the sum of every possible way in: the smaller it is, the fewer chances an attacker has.

## Lesson: l0401 | Threat actors and their motivations

### Attributes used to compare actors

- **Internal or external**: is the actor inside the organization (an employee, contractor) or outside it? Insiders already have access and knowledge.
- **Resources and funding**: from a lone person with a laptop to a government agency with large budgets and teams.
- **Level of sophistication and capability**: from running ready-made tools to writing custom malware and using unknown (zero-day) vulnerabilities.

### The actors

- **Nation-state**: a government or a group it sponsors. Very well funded and highly skilled; patient, long-term operations, often called an **advanced persistent threat** (APT) because they stay hidden in a network for months. Aims: espionage, war, disruption of another country's infrastructure, sometimes money or influence.
- **Unskilled attacker** (once called a "script kiddie"): little skill, uses tools and scripts others wrote. Limited resources, but can still do real harm with easy targets. Often driven by curiosity, bragging or chaos.
- **Hacktivist**: attacks to promote a cause, political or social. Typical methods: defacing websites, leaking documents, denial-of-service. Skill and funding vary.
- **Insider threat**: a current or former employee, contractor or partner who misuses their access, on purpose (revenge, money) or by accident. Hard to spot because their access looks normal.
- **Organized crime**: criminal groups in it for money: ransomware, fraud, stolen data sold online. Well funded and skilled, run like businesses.
- **Shadow IT**: staff using hardware, software or cloud services without IT's approval, such as a team storing customer files in a personal cloud account. Usually not malicious, but it creates unmanaged, unprotected systems: an internal actor that adds risk.

> Most funded and most patient: nation-state (APT). Least skill: unskilled attacker. A cause: hacktivist. Money: organized crime.

### Motivations

- **Data exfiltration**: stealing data to sell or use.
- **Espionage**: spying for a government or a competitor.
- **Service disruption**: taking systems offline.
- **Blackmail**: threatening to publish data or keep systems locked unless paid (as in ransomware with data leak threats).
- **Financial gain**: direct profit through fraud, ransom or theft.
- **Philosophical or political beliefs**: the hacktivist's cause.
- **Ethical**: "white hat" hackers who find flaws to get them fixed, ideally with permission or through a disclosure program.
- **Revenge**: a fired or unhappy employee hitting back.
- **Disruption or chaos**: causing damage just to cause it.
- **War**: cyber attacks as part of military conflict, such as attacks on power grids or communications.

## Lesson: l0402 | Threat vectors and attack surfaces

### Message-based vectors

- **Email**: the most common way in: malicious links, attachments, fake invoices.
- **SMS** (text messages) and **instant messaging** (chat apps, workplace chat): links and requests that look urgent, often on phones with fewer protections.

### Image-based and file-based vectors

- **Image-based**: malicious code or links hidden in images (for example a QR code leading to a fake site, or an image file crafted to exploit a viewer bug). Steganography can hide data in images too.
- **File-based**: documents with malicious macros, PDFs exploiting reader flaws, archives containing malware, script files disguised as documents.

### Voice call

Phone calls (and VoIP) used to trick people into giving information or access: vishing (next lesson). Caller ID can be faked.

### Removable devices

USB drives left in a car park ("baiting"), malicious USB cables or devices that act like keyboards. Removable media can also carry data out. Defences: block or control USB storage, disable autorun, train staff not to plug in found devices.

### Vulnerable software

- **Client-based** software needs an agent or program installed on the device (for example a VPN client or a management agent); a flaw in it puts every device that runs it at risk, and each copy must be patched.
- **Agentless** approaches use no installed client (for example a web browser to reach a web app, or scanning without an agent). There is less installed software to attack, but the server side and the browser are still targets.

Security+ uses "client-based vs agentless" mainly to describe where the vulnerable code lives and what has to be maintained.

### Unsupported systems and applications

Software past its **end of life** gets no more security patches. Every new flaw found in it stays open forever. Replace it, or isolate it with compensating controls.

### Unsecure networks

- **Wireless**: open or weakly protected Wi-Fi (WEP, WPA with weak passphrases), rogue access points, evil twins.
- **Wired**: unused network ports left active in public areas let anyone plug in; use 802.1X and disable unused ports.
- **Bluetooth**: discoverable devices can be targeted (bluejacking: sending unwanted messages; bluesnarfing: stealing data); keep devices non-discoverable and patched.

### Open service ports

Every listening service is a door. Services that aren't needed, or that are exposed to the internet when they needn't be (remote desktop, database ports, old file-sharing protocols), widen the attack surface. Close unused ports and limit the rest with firewalls.

### Default credentials

Devices and applications often ship with known usernames and passwords (`admin` / `admin`). Lists of defaults are public, so attackers try them first, especially on routers, cameras and IoT devices. Change them before the device goes live.

### Supply chain

Attackers may go through a trusted partner instead of attacking you directly:

- **Managed service providers (MSPs)**: they often have remote admin access to many customers at once, so one breached MSP can open many doors.
- **Vendors**: software or hardware vendors can ship compromised updates or products.
- **Suppliers**: companies that supply parts, services or materials can be the weak link (for example a heating contractor with network access).

> Shrinking the attack surface: remove unused software and services, close ports, change defaults, replace unsupported systems, control removable media.

## Lesson: l0403 | Social engineering and human vectors

**Social engineering** manipulates people into giving up information, money or access. It works by using trust, fear, urgency, authority, helpfulness and curiosity. Technical controls help, but awareness training is the main defence.

### Phishing and its variants

- **Phishing**: fraudulent messages (usually email) that try to get the reader to click a link, open an attachment or hand over credentials. Mass phishing goes to many people; **spear phishing** targets a specific person or group with personal details; **whaling** targets senior executives.
- **Vishing** (voice phishing): the same tricks by phone. "This is the bank's fraud team, please confirm your card number."
- **Smishing** (SMS phishing): by text message. "Your parcel is held, pay the fee here."

### Pretending to be someone

- **Impersonation**: the attacker poses as someone trusted: a colleague, IT support, a manager, a delivery driver.
- **Pretexting**: the attacker builds a believable story (the pretext) to justify the request. "I'm from the auditors, and I need the vendor list before the meeting at 3." Pretexting is the scenario; impersonation is often part of it.
- **Business email compromise (BEC)**: the attacker takes over, or convincingly imitates, a real business email account (often an executive's or a supplier's) and asks for payments or sensitive data. Because the message comes from a real or very familiar address, it often has no malware or bad link at all, which helps it pass filters. Classic example: "Please change our bank details for the next invoice."
- **Brand impersonation**: messages and sites copying a well-known brand's logos and style to win trust.

### Fake places and fake information

- **Typosquatting** (URL hijacking): registering domain names that are easy typos or look-alikes of real ones, such as `examp1e.com` for `example.com`, to catch people who mistype or don't look closely.
- **Watering hole attack**: instead of attacking the target directly, the attacker compromises a website the target group often visits (a trade association site, a local forum) and plants malware there, then waits for victims to come.
- **Misinformation**: false information spread without the intent to deceive (people simply believe it). **Disinformation**: false information spread on purpose to deceive or cause harm, for example a fake announcement to move a share price or damage a reputation.

> BEC often has no link or attachment: just a convincing request from a real or look-alike account. Verify payment changes by phoning a known number.

```file
From: "Dana Whitlock (CFO)" <dana.whitlock@examp1e-corp.com>
To: accounts@example-corp.com
Subject: Urgent - supplier payment today

Hi, I'm in meetings all afternoon. Please wire 48,500 to the new account
below for Lakeside Supplies before 4pm and keep this between us for now.
```

The look-alike domain (`examp1e-corp.com`, with the digit 1), the urgency, the secrecy and the change of payment details are all warning signs.

### Defences

- Security awareness training and phishing simulations.
- Verify unusual requests through a separate, known channel (call back on a number you already have).
- Email security: filtering, warnings on external mail, SPF, DKIM and DMARC.
- MFA, so stolen passwords alone are not enough.
- Clear processes for payment changes and password resets that can't be skipped "because it's urgent".
